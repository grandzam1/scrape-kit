Elon Musk has been taking big swings this year. After merging his artificial intelligence lab xAI with his rocket company SpaceX, Musk took the combined business public in June, in the largest IPO in history, with a record $1.7 trillion valuation.

SpaceX then announced plans to acquire the popular AI coding startup Cursor for $60 billion, in a deal that would hand the company key firepower in perhaps the most strategically vital AI capability of them all.

In August, Musk’s AI lab, now a subsidiary called SpaceXAI, released its top model, [Grok 4.6](https://x.ai/news/grok-4-6), with a focus on long-running agents and “more ambitious interactive and visual work” at a competitive price. In January, it brought in stronger safeguards two weeks after it emerged that Grok was being [used to create](https://time.com/7344858/grok-deepfake-crisis-explained/) nonconsensual sexual images of women and children. In the most recent AI [safety rankings](https://futureoflife.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/07/AI-Safety-Index-Report_010726_2Pager.pdf) published by the Future of Life Institute, a nonprofit that researches and advocates against large-scale risks from technology, xAI’s rank dropped to seventh of nine model makers, none of whom scored above C+. (SpaceX did not respond to a request for comment for this article.)

Musk has long been deeply interested in the future of AI. He was an early investor in DeepMind and a co-founder and donor to OpenAI due to his concerns about the technology’s existential risks, he said at the time. Ultimately, his relationship with his OpenAI co-founders soured and in May his long-running lawsuit against the AI lab was [dismissed](https://time.com/article/2026/05/19/elon-musk-openai-trial-xai-jury-verdict/). Today, his view is less clear. “Will this be bad or good for humanity? I think it’ll be good. Most likely it’ll be good,” Musk [said](https://time.com/7301736/ai-redistribution-profits/) of AI at a launch event for a Grok model last year. “But I’ve somewhat reconciled myself to the fact that even if it wasn’t gonna be good, I’d at least like to be alive to see it happen.”

# Mark Chen, Sam Altman, and Greg Brockman

Chief Research Officer, OpenAI; CEO, OpenAI; President, OpenAI

![](https://static.time.com/v3/assets/bltea6093859af6183b/blt85625e6f4bc6a095/6a8ef5e52a9d088a9ca716eb/Altman_-Brockman_-Chen.jpg?branch=production&width=3840&quality=75&auto=webp&crop=3:2)

Jessica Chou for TIME

It’s been a difficult stretch for the company that ushered in the AI boom. It dealt with leadership departures, rogue agents, a public backlash against the industry, and stiff competition from rival Anthropic, whose success in the coding and enterprise markets lifted its value above OpenAI's as the two companies prepare for expected IPOs. But OpenAI’s leaders are optimistic that they have fixed the product and operational missteps that enabled Anthropic to seize the lead in the AI race. Their vision for the company’s future is audacious: state-of-the-art models; superintelligent personal assistants; new chips, data centers, and consumer devices; and a renewed emphasis on safety and alignment that executives hope will set new industry standards and spur broader cooperation. While Altman remains the CEO and public face, OpenAI co-founder Brockman is now running large parts of the company, from revenue to product marketing. And Chen, the chief research officer, oversees teams that, by his estimate, have brought OpenAI “80% of the way” to achieving artificial general intelligence—the company’s founding goal, but no longer its lodestar. As Brockman puts it: “We are looking at transforming the entire economy.”

After the 2022 release of ChatGPT, OpenAI was the unquestionable frontrunner of the AI race. But over the last year, Anthropic—led by brother-and-sister duo Dario and Daniela Amodei—has surpassed OpenAI in both its valuation and revenue, thanks to its new Claude models.

Claude Code, a version of Anthropic’s model specially designed for coding, has upturned software engineering and turbocharged Anthropic’s growth. The model can design and debug working programs to written specifications, often faster and better than humans.

As their models get progressively powerful, Dario and Daniela Amodei have also increasingly become embroiled in geopolitical matters. In the spring, Anthropic [clashed](https://time.com/article/2026/03/11/anthropic-claude-disruptive-company-pentagon/) with the Pentagon over the company’s limits on how its models could be used by the U.S. military. Tensions continued when the Trump Administration placed export controls on Anthropic’s powerful Mythos model, which is capable of finding and exploiting thousands of critical vulnerabilities in commonly-used software, as well as its more constrained sister model Fable 5.

Despite the scrutiny these issues have brought upon Anthropic, its leaders welcome the idea of policymakers considering regulating AI, a technology that for years they largely ignored. In an Aug. 15 [post on X](https://x.com/DarioAmodei/status/2088758816376807762?s=20), Dario Amodei expressed support for “pre-deployment testing for frontier models, and also testing of open-weights models when they get closer to the frontier” as well as a self-regulatory body for AI.

[IBM](https://time.com/collection/time100-most-influential-companies/2026/ibm/) chief executive Arvind Krishna has a lot on his mind. “Executive orders. A dynamic market. Where’s the AI trade? What do clients want to do? The end of the quarter, product innovation. Name it,” he says.

Krishna became the company’s CEO and chairman in 2020, just in time for the AI boom. To him, steering a 115-year-old technology giant through that upheaval requires being bold. “I actually think that trying to muddle along without taking risk is the riskiest path,” he says. “It leads to eventual falling off a cliff.”

IBM trains small, efficient models of its own—some of which run on its mainframe computers that many top banks and other enterprise clients still rely on. If a client’s application needs a larger model, IBM rents one from Google, OpenAI, or Anthropic. Krishna’s central bet is that, in the end, models will become commodities. That doesn’t mean they’re not valuable. “What I mean is that you can use any one of them, because they’re close enough,” he says. Krishna believes that 90% of the value will eventually accrue not to the companies training large AI models, but companies like IBM that build applications on top.

His prediction is yet to fully pan out. Though IBM’s shares have nearly doubled since Krishna took the reins, they tumbled 25% in one day in July as customers shifted spending from mainframes to AI infrastructure and have yet to recover to their highs. “We did not adapt and move quickly enough,” Krishna [wrote](https://newsroom.ibm.com/2026-07-14-Arvind-Krishnas-Letter-to-IBM-Investors?mod=djemCEO) to investors. Meanwhile, leading model builders have swallowed some of the most successful AI-native application startups, like Cursor and Windsurf.

“We are not quite there yet,” Krishna says. But he believes proprietary data will be a differentiating factor rather than raw model-power, in which case trust becomes key. He believes IBM’s reputation working with enterprises in highly regulated sectors will be an advantage.

Lila Ibrahim was founding chief operating officer at [Google DeepMind](https://time.com/collections/time100-companies-2025/7289661/google-deepmind/) for nearly eight years. But in January she stepped into a new role: helping to prepare the world for rapidly advancing artificial intelligence. “We’re never going to have as much time to prepare as now, with the rate of how the technology is being developed,” Ibrahim tells TIME. “How do we ensure that we’re building the technology for and with communities, so it doesn’t just happen to them?”

Her role involves looking into the future to anticipate the potential capabilities of the technology, ensuring that her teams have researched their implications, and being ready to test and help set policies once the possibilities become reality. That doesn’t just mean implementing the learnings at Google, but also working with governments’ AI safety institutes around the world, as well as other organizations, scientists, and economists.

Ibrahim believes that some day every AI company will have a chief AI readiness officer, tasked with navigating between the technology’s potential opportunities and risks. Responsibility and safety “can’t be an afterthought,” she says.

Ibrahim, who has previously worked at Intel, Kleiner Perkins, and Coursera, is acutely aware that the stakes are high. “I’ve seen in my career what happens when we get technology right, and what happens when we don’t,” she says. “If we want AI to have \[a\] positive impact in the world, we need to take the extra steps in trying to mitigate the risks and keeping the trust that we've worked so hard to to build.”

The Trump Administration is growing increasingly concerned about AI, especially variants with dangerous cybersecurity capabilities. Along with the rebrand has come a shift in focus. It does so by developing and carrying out tests that can measure frontier models’ capabilities, and their dangers. CAISI then shares its learnings from these tests with other parts of government.

The focus, though, is squarely on helping AI companies move faster. “We are not regulatory by any means,” Raman says. “Our purpose is to help the industry.”

Moustapha Cissé hadn’t planned on launching a revolutionary AI health insurance company in Senegal when he raised his first $3 million pre-seed round, he says. The former Facebook and Google AI researcher’s big idea then was to build a universal platform to gather disparate health records so doctors could diagnose and treat illnesses more efficiently.

Yet Cissé, who also founded the continent’s first master’s program in machine learning at the African Institute of Mathematical Sciences, couldn’t get cooperation from existing insurance carriers. So in September 2024, he launched a new, transformative one, Sura, under his startup Kera Health Platforms. The company raised $10 million from the International Finance Corporation last year and has continued to raise more, Cissé says.

Sura uses AI to analyze records and approve claims within 30 seconds—a striking shift in a country where providers usually wait 120 days to be paid, Cissé says. That delay is why a wide swath of Senegalese can’t see doctors: many people can’t afford to pay out of pocket, and many providers can’t afford to wait three months to be paid. “This is a structural health problem because it significantly reduces access, especially for those who need it most,” says Cissé. With Sura, AI never rejects a claim; it just sends it to humans to review.

Tens of thousands of people are now covered by Sura, which launched in the Ivory Coast earlier this year, a third African nation is coming in the fall, Cissé says. He wants to expand into the U.S. and elsewhere, “because the need is the same everywhere,” Cissé says. There’s no app and not much of a website, either; all transactions and interactions take place in WhatsApp.

It also partnered with the clinician network Better Evidence this year for usage at the front lines of the Ebola crisis, Nadler says.

Widespread adoption has led OpenEvidence to become one of the hottest AI-related startups. The company closed a $250 million funding round in January led by Thrive Capital and DST, valuing it at $12 billion.

Founder Nadler says that he’s less excited about OpenEvidence’s financial success than its impact. “It’s making it so that a physician practicing in rural southwestern Georgia with a 75% African American population making $43,000 a year can nevertheless have access to an elastically expandable panel of subspecialists,” says Nadler. “It is leveling the delivery of high-quality health care in the United States.” But he emphasizes that it can’t replace doctors.

There are plenty of reasons to be skeptical about health-tech startups promising big things—something that Nadler is acutely aware of. “If you analyze the last 50 years of big hospital systems, there’s this graveyard of tools that have never been used by doctors that the hospital systems paid seven or eight figures for,” he says.

OpenEvidence faces competition from OpenAI and Anthropic’s health offerings, but it has a head start in winning over the medical community. Nadler hopes that even amid AI debates, skeptics can see the benefits of a tool like his. “It’s such a natural, obvious application of AI,” he says.

The company says it topped $100 million in annualized revenue last year, driven mostly by selling ads to pharmaceutical companies. Nadler is adamant that he wants OpenEvidence to be free for users.
